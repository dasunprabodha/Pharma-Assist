Prescription Reader – AI-assisted prescription reading and dispensing check

IT6070 Emerging Topics in IT · Assignment II · U.W.D.P. Amarasinghe – MS25903874 · Technical evidence · 18/10/2026 · Repository: [add link]

1. Professional and problem

"Pharmacist A" (declared pseudonym; real name withheld), Registered Pharmacist, Dulnitha Pharmacy, Kottawa – an independent community retail pharmacy (healthcare); pharmacy named with permission. The pharmacist dispenses prescriptions at the counter, most of them handwritten by different doctors. Evidence of need (face-to-face discussion, 30/09/2026; pharmacist's estimates): ~200 prescriptions/day, 70–80% handwritten; 2–3 min each to decipher, cross-check and match to stock (≈5–8 staff-hours/day); ~3% need a colleague or the prescriber. Misreading a drug or dose is a patient-safety risk.

  • R1 – Read the prescription: from a phone photo, list each item's drug, strength, dose, frequency and duration in an editable list within 30 s, expanding shorthand (bd, nocte, 5/7) and flagging uncertain fields.
  • R2 – Check before dispensing: match the confirmed list with stock and interaction rules; produce a checklist with warnings and in-stock generic alternatives. No patient name is sent or stored.

2. Emerging technology, justification and alternative

A multimodal (vision) foundation model – Amazon Nova 2 Lite on Amazon Bedrock – looks at the photo and returns the items as structured data in one step (Bedrock Converse API; image + prompt; a forced record_prescription tool fixes the answer format; temperature 0). It understands context and shorthand, reports a confidence per item, needs no training data, runs in ≈2.5 s and costs ≈ LKR 0.33 per scan. The model is a setting, so it can be changed without code. Alternative: Amazon Textract (handwriting OCR) → Amazon Comprehend Medical (entity extraction) → rules. Against R1 it needs two separate services, OCR mistakes flow straight into the extraction, and Comprehend Medical is tuned to US clinical text and drug names, so Sri Lankan brands and shorthand (e.g. "1×2", "5/7") may be missed. Nova met R1 in one configurable step and was chosen; both options keep the app AWS-only.

3. Architecture

Orange = external AWS service; purple dashed = simulated data; yellow = fallback. PoC runs locally; production would use one AWS Lightsail server. Details: docs/architecture.md.

4. Two connected features and their role in the workflow

Feature 1 – Read the prescription (R1). Upload or photograph a prescription (JPG/PNG/WEBP or PDF ≤ 3 pages). The server checks the file, removes all metadata and sends it to Nova; the items appear in an editable table beside the zoomable photo. Low-confidence rows are listed ("Row 2: low confidence – please verify"), each name shows its stock match and pack image, "Did you mean…?" chips suggest stock names, and corrections are counted. Fallback: every AI error (not configured, unavailable, unreadable, invalid result) shows a clear message and opens manual entry, which produces the same list.
Feature 2 – Check before dispensing (R2). The confirmed list – from the AI or manual entry – is checked against the stock list and rules: the pack to dispense (image), stock status, in-stock alternatives with the same active ingredient, interaction / duplicate-class / duplicate-ingredient warnings (major → moderate → minor), unknown items flagged "not checked", strength mismatches noted, a Checked box per item and a CSV checklist. It turns Feature 1's output into the pharmacist's dispensing decision.

Critical evaluation. 82 automated tests pass (Bedrock mocked). On 9 redacted Sri Lankan prescriptions (33 items) Nova found 76% of items and read 52% of drug names and 42% of fields correctly, but flagged 100% of the items it got wrong (2.4 s/scan). Clear handwriting was read perfectly and repeatably (TC1); dense cursive was not, the model sometimes added a duration that was not written and could miss an item without a flag. Three prompt versions and the larger Nova Pro did not improve accuracy. Improvements: a pilot with more local samples, per-line cropping, and showing which part of the photo each field came from.

8. Benefits, limitations, risks and adoption

Benefits: fast (≈2.5 s vs 2–3 min); cheap; flags its own uncertain items; structured, auditable lists; automatic stock, alternative and interaction checks; pack images help pick the right product.

Limitations: weak on dense cursive handwriting; may add an unwritten duration or miss an item; small test set (n = 9 + synthetic); simulated stock and rules; no live stock-system link; no login yet; internet required.

Risks → mitigations: misread drug or dose → pharmacist confirms every item, flags, photo side by side, decision-support label; patient-data exposure → metadata removed, nothing stored, redaction, PDPA review; AI outage → automatic manual entry; surprise bill → AWS budget alert.

Adoption: the pharmacy owner pays (≈ LKR 25,000 setup + ≈ LKR 5,900/month). Start with a two-week pilot at the counter: the dashboard records read success and corrections, and staff time prescriptions with and without the tool. Main condition to proceed: measured accuracy and a time saving above the 16-second break-even.
